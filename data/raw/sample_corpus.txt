The lighthouse keeper woke before dawn, as she always did. The sea was calm.
The lighthouse keeper woke before dawn, as she always did. The sea was rough.
The lighthouse keeper woke before dawn, as she always did. The sea was quiet.

She climbed the spiral stairs, one hand on the cold iron rail. The lamp room
smelled of oil and salt. She trimmed the wick. She polished the glass. She
checked the log book and wrote the date, the wind, the weather.

The lighthouse keeper loved the lighthouse. The lighthouse loved the sea.
The sea did not love anything. The sea simply was.

At noon the keeper walked down to the rocks and watched the gulls. The gulls
did not care about the lighthouse. The gulls cared about fish.

At dusk the keeper climbed the stairs again and lit the lamp. The light
turned, slow and steady, out over the water. Ships far away saw the light
and knew where the rocks were. That was the whole job. Warn the ships. Keep
the light turning. Write it in the book.

The lighthouse keeper wrote in the book every night: the wind, the weather,
the ships that passed, the ships that did not. Some nights nothing happened.
Some nights a storm came in fast and the keeper stayed awake watching the
light, making sure it never stopped turning.

The lighthouse keeper woke before dawn, as she always did. The sea was calm.
She climbed the spiral stairs, one hand on the cold iron rail. She trimmed
the wick. She polished the glass. She wrote the date, the wind, the weather.

Years passed. The keeper grew old. The lighthouse did not grow old, or if it
did, it did not show it, the way stone does not show it. The lamp still
turned. The light still warned the ships. The book still filled with dates
and weather and the names of ships that passed in the night.

One winter the keeper's daughter came to learn the job. The keeper showed
her the spiral stairs, the lamp room, the oil and the wick and the glass.
The keeper showed her the log book, years of it, shelf after shelf. Warn the
ships. Keep the light turning. Write it in the book.

The daughter climbed the stairs before dawn, as she would always do now.
The sea was calm. The sea was rough. The sea was quiet. The lighthouse did
not care which. The lighthouse only turned its light, steady, out over the
water, the way it always had, the way it always would.
